Ananias and Sapphira

2025 essay

Every system of thought confronts the question of final authority. Any coherent intellectual framework requires an ultimate arbiter, an endpoint of justification beyond which appeal cannot proceed. In Christianity, this end is divine revelation. In empirical science, it is observational evidence constrained by logical consistency. In constitutional law, it is the text as interpreted through established precedent. What counts as final authority differs between systems, but every system needs one. Without an ultimate stopping point, we fall into infinite regress — endlessly justifying each principle by appeal to another principle, which itself requires justification, and so on without end. The critical question emerges when the system's demands conflict with private judgment. Who decides when Scripture commands what strikes you as unreasonable? Who adjudicates when teaching contradicts your moral intuitions? The answer reveals where authority actually resides, regardless of what we profess to believe. If you consistently privilege private judgment whenever conflict arises, then private judgment functions as your final authority, whatever claims you make about submitting to external revelation.

Ananias and Sapphira claimed total submission while reserving final authority to themselves. They sold property, delivered proceeds to the apostles, participated in the community's ritual structure. When the moment arrived to choose between the system's demand and private preference, private judgment prevailed. The subtlety of their crime lay in its resemblance to genuine participation. They did not openly defy apostolic authority or refuse donation. They performed submission while retaining ultimate authority over what that submission required. They wanted recognition as people for whom God's authority was final while keeping themselves as final arbiters of their own actions. They determined what counted as acceptable devotion, then declared their determination equivalent to total devotion. This epistemological reservation — the retention of final judgment — produced a deeper structural inversion.

Augustine distinguished two fundamental orientations: uti (use) and frui (enjoyment). The soul must enjoy God alone as ultimate end, final good, end of desire. Everything else — religious practice, community, Scripture itself — exists as means toward that ultimate enjoyment. When this structure inverts, when created goods become objects of enjoyment and God becomes instrumental to securing those goods, the sacred order collapses. Ananias and Sapphira executed this inversion precisely. They enjoyed their performance — the recognition, the status, the appearance of radical commitment. They used God as validating authority for that performance. Divine reality became instrumental to social standing. The act of submission became the end enjoyed, rather than means toward enjoying God Himself. The structure inverted completely, and complete inversion required death.

The mercy we experience is that God does not execute the same judgment on contemporary Christians who commit identical inversions. We fall into this pattern repeatedly, often without recognition. Consider the times you evaluated your spiritual life by comparing your devotional practices to others. Consider when you felt satisfaction not from encountering God but from being seen as someone who encounters God. Consider when theological precision became about winning arguments rather than submitting to truth. Consider when worship generated pleasure through aesthetic experience rather than through self-offering to divine reality. Consider when your commitment to justice, or purity, or correct doctrine became the good you enjoyed while God became the authority invoked to validate that commitment. The inversion operates everywhere. We enjoy the identity of being theologically serious Christians. We enjoy the community of like-minded believers. We enjoy the moral clarity of our positions. We enjoy the aesthetic richness of liturgy or the emotional intensity of contemporary worship. And we use God — His authority, His validation, His blessing — as means to secure and justify these enjoyments.

I too am not exempt from this pattern. I recognize the inversion in myself constantly. I admit to feeling the enjoyment of theological precision pursued for its own sake rather than as means toward God Himself. I catch myself treating the act of theological analysis as an end I enjoy while God becomes the subject matter that validates my intellectual activity. The structure remains inverted in me as thoroughly as in anyone.

The proper response to such recognition should be what Acts records: "Great fear seized the whole church and all who heard about these events." Not shame at moral failure, but epistemological terror — the recognition that objective reality exists independent of our performance, that divine knowledge cannot be manipulated through carefully managed appearances, that God knows what we have actually done regardless of what we claim. Yet we experience no such fear. I confess the inversion and continue writing. You recognize it and continue reading. Like many other teachings of God, we acknowledge the pattern without the terror that should accompany such acknowledgment. The absence reveals something more fundamental than individual moral weakness. It exposes our functional constructivism.

We do not actually believe that God knows reality independently of what we tell Him. We do not believe divine judgment operates beyond the terms we construct. We have adopted an epistemological framework in which fear of objective divine knowledge becomes impossible. Fear requires belief in reality that exists independent of human construction. It requires conviction that truth remains true regardless of our interpretation, that divine authority functions whether or not we recognize it, that spiritual reality has determinate structure we cannot negotiate through performance. But contemporary Christianity has absorbed the premises that make such convictions unintelligible. When final judgment resides with individual interpretation, when God's authority extends only as far as we permit it through our hermeneutics, we have eliminated the conditions that make fear possible.

Perhaps after the death of truth, even witnessing death itself proves insufficient. The early church feared because they recognized death as divine judgment revealing objective reality. We lack that framework. Death has been stripped of its revelatory capacity — it no longer exposes truth. We hear repeatedly about divine judgment, about consequences for sin, about the holiness that our actions violate. We acknowledge these warnings intellectually. Yet we continue the inversions without fear, often without even shame. Ananias and Sapphira were executed to prevent the normalization of epistemological reservation and structural inversion. Their deaths accomplished nothing. We have become what they pretended to be.
