Compression of Time and Faith

Jun 2026 essay

When we read the Bible, we are inevitably hindered by the limitations of language, a medium that traps us in a profound "retrospective illusion." Søren Kierkegaard exposes this cognitive blind spot in his examination of Abraham and Isaac, reminding us how easily we bypass the agonizing human nature and psychological torment these figures actually endured.

Kierkegaard places a heavy emphasis on the three-day journey Abraham made with Isaac to Mount Moriah. During this period, no matter how much we claim to sympathize with Abraham's torment, we simply cannot reasonably comprehend it. Sure, we can easily recognize the physical reality of a three-day journey and intellectually grasp how the text compresses that time. Yet no human words or comparable travel experiences can ever give us a true sense of the paradoxical crossroads of contemplation that Abraham was forced to endure. Moreover, the preceding circumstances make this decision and leap of faith unimaginably extraordinary. We must remember the fact that Isaac was the miraculous fulfillment of God's own promise for the future generations, and Abraham knew this. So, even the torment of doubt and trying to navigate a decision that aligns with God's promise and his orders, is also unlike anything we will go through as Christians. Yet, we read over this journey in mere seconds, rushing to the resolution of the ram in the thicket, and then casually praying to mimic the faith that Abraham had to raise the knife on his own son. Only by looking deeply into the terrifying details of those seventy-two hours do we see how impossible that faith truly is, and how much suffering actually goes into it. Again, even if we claim to understand after studying the text deeply, we simply do not. We objectively cannot access the subjective experience of Abraham. Not only is a similar situation most certainly infeasible for us to encounter, but the text itself cannot do justice to the amount of strain and suffering a human must endure to pursue such a profound faith. The fault does not lie in the sacred text itself, but in the structural boundaries of language, which inherently fails to convey the crushing weight of time and suffering, and our human constraints in cognition that does not let us put ourselves to feel certain situations without actually being in them.

When we fail to recognize this boundary, the compression of time and events within the text, mixed with our cognitive constraints, leads to profound problems of faith. We end up systematically deforming our theology of what belief actually requires. The nature of how we read through the text dictates our understanding of spiritual reality, and in this case, with no deep critical thought into the missing parts of the biblical stories, we create an epistemological shortcut. We substitute the rapid consumption of the narrative for the agonizing endurance of actual belief, producing two distinct distortions in how contemporary Christians approach God.

The first distortion is the reduction of faith from a sustained duration into a momentary decision. Because the text artificially accelerates time, it psychologically tricks our constrained cognition into defining faith as a singular, heroic event. We imagine Abraham making a radical choice at the moment of God's command, locking it in, and simply executing it at the summit. But in the uncompressed reality of human time, faith cannot be decided once and stored safely away. It must be sustained. For seventy-two hours, with every step taken toward Moriah, with every meal eaten in silence, and with every hour spent awake in the dark, the decision had to be agonizingly remade. By compressing the time, we compress the definition of faith itself. We reduce it from a continuous, crushing exertion of the will into a mere transactional leap. We demand the aesthetic of the leap, but we are entirely unequipped for the terrifying, uncompressed duration of the walk.

Consequently, this leads to a second structural problem. We deform our spiritual expectations through narrative velocity. The speed at which our eyes move from the terrifying command in verse 2 to the miraculous resolution in verse 13 creates the cognitive illusion that divine paradoxes are quickly resolved. But absolute faith exists strictly in real-time. When we subject our theology to this narrative compression, we strip faith of its primary refining mechanism. We remove the experience of waiting in the dark without an answer. We construct a framework where spiritual maturity is measured by how quickly we arrive at divine clarity, entirely missing the reality that Abraham's faith was forged precisely in the agonizing, slow-motion absence of that clarity. To compress the time is to annihilate the very conditions that make such faith possible. No wonder we become frustrated with God's silence in our own lives. Our interpretations have been falsely conditioned by our limited capacity to understand scripture, causing us to expect immediate narrative resolution.

When this artificially constructed expectation collides with the unyielding reality of human time, the modern Christian experiences an epistemological crisis. Because we expect the swift resolution of the text, prolonged periods of spiritual silence or suffering feel like divine abandonment. We lack the theological categories to process a God who demands we walk in the dark for days, months, or years without a guaranteed outcome. And it is precisely in this gap — the space between our expectation of speed and the reality of delay — that our functional constructivism reveals itself.

Unable to bear the uncompressed tension of the journey, we manufacture our own resolution. We construct our own "rams in the thicket." When the divine answer does not arrive according to our timeline, we lower our ethical standards, choose the path of least resistance, or force a premature conclusion, and then we baptize our impatience as divine providence. We declare that God has "opened a door" or "provided a way," when in reality, we have simply abandoned the path to Moriah because the silence became too heavy to carry. We substitute our own constructed resolution for the objective will of God, desperately attempting to relieve the psychological pressure of a paradox we were never trained to endure.

This reveals a catastrophic weakening of the modern Christian will. We have built spiritual frameworks entirely dependent on aesthetic intensity and rapid emotional payoff. But the road to Mount Moriah offers no aesthetic appeal. It is completely barren of emotional validation. If faith is not sustained by an underlying emotional high or the promise of an immediate breakthrough, and if it instead requires sheer intentionality where the mind reaches toward an objective God despite the total absence of phenomenal confirmation, then most contemporary faith is exposed as disastrously fragile. We have unlearned the discipline of spiritual endurance because we have forgotten how to read the silence between the verses.

Therefore, we must profoundly shift our posture toward how we read scripture. It is not a therapeutic roadmap for our own success. It is an existential anomaly that should shatter our rational categories and dismantle our demands. Sure, there are no concrete ways to deal with the struggles of knowing what is part of God's plan in the moment or if something is truly what God is calling you to do. But the best thing we can do is finally recognize our limitations. We must surrender the arrogant assumption that we can figure out God's plan, and we must stop blindly asking for the triumphant conclusion of faith that Abraham exhibited after raising his knife. We are meant to stand at the bottom of the mountain, looking up at the silent summit, gripped by the sheer epistemological terror of what absolute obedience to objective divine reality actually requires without knowing what happens next. Only when we allow the text to remain unbridgeable, incomprehensible, and agonizingly slow can we begin to approach God with the humility, fear, and trembling that true faith demands.
