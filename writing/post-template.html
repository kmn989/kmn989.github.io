The Tyranny of Optimization

May 2026 essay

There's a particular kind of person who has optimized their life so thoroughly that they've stopped living it. They track their sleep to the minute, batch their social interactions to protect "deep work" time, and have outsourced their reading list to a Goodreads algorithm. Everything is a system. Nothing is an accident.

I used to admire this. Now I find it quietly terrifying.

The case for optimization is obvious and well-worn: you have limited time, limited energy, limited attention. Waste less of it. Sleep eight hours. Exercise consistently. Eat the same thing for lunch. Read for an hour before bed. These are not bad habits. The problem isn't the habits — it's the posture behind them.

The thing optimization misses

Optimization assumes you already know what to optimize for. But most of the best things in my life — the books that actually changed me, the friendships that went deep, the ideas I still think about years later — arrived through some combination of accident, inefficiency, and what looked at the time like wasted time.

I learned more about how organizations work by getting lunch with someone I had no reason to meet than I did from any book on organizational behavior. I found the essay that reframed my whole understanding of ambition because a friend texted it to me at midnight on a Tuesday when I should have been asleep.

The scheduled, the deliberate, the optimized — these things compound. But so does the serendipitous. And the serendipitous requires slack.

Slack is the enemy of optimization. It is also, I think, the precondition for discovery.

What I'm actually arguing

I'm not making the case for chaos or deliberate inefficiency. I'm making the case for being honest about what optimization can and can't do. It can help you execute on a known goal. It cannot help you find the goal worth executing on. That requires something slower, more uncertain, more conversational — with other people, with books, with yourself.

The tyranny of optimization is that it colonizes the slack. It makes you feel guilty for reading something off your list, talking to someone who isn't professionally useful, or following a thought somewhere unexpected. It treats every unscheduled hour as a failure of planning rather than an invitation.

I want to be a person who plans well and wanders freely. I'm not sure the second is possible if the first becomes total.
