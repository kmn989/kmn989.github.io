The Death of Truth

2025 essay

Philosophers from Descartes through Kant pursued a universal foundation for truth independent of confessional Christian commitments. They wanted to find a framework of rational principles accessible to any thinking mind, regardless of religious tradition. They thought if divisive theological claims could be bracketed, reason itself might furnish the moral and metaphysical certainties that faith had previously supplied. Yet this project contained a fatal vulnerability that its architects failed to anticipate. The attempt to ground knowledge in individual rational consciousness inevitably exposed philosophy to an inescapable problem. Once scripture, tradition, and external revelation were set aside, only the individual mind remained as the locus of certainty. But Descartes discovered that the thinking self, isolated in its own consciousness, cannot escape its own perceptions. His successors (Locke, Hume, Kant) each wrestled with the same difficulty. How does a mind that knows only its own ideas gain access to a world beyond those ideas? How can subjective experience yield objective knowledge? How can the particular consciousness of the individual philosopher generate universal principles binding on all rational beings?

The answer, as the tradition from Hume through Nietzsche demonstrated, is that it cannot. Each attempt to derive universal truth from individual reason exposed the same structural problem: a mind examining only its own contents cannot verify that those contents correspond to external reality. Hume concluded that causation itself was merely a habit of expectation, not a feature of the world. Kant attempted to rescue universality by locating it in the structure of mind itself, but this merely relocated the problem. The hope of replacing faith with reason foundered on the recognition that reason, cut off from any access to objective reality, cannot generate the certainties it promised. What followed was not merely the loss of religious authority but the collapse of the entire category of universal truth. Once philosophy conceded that the mind cannot grasp reality independently of its own constructive activity, the basis for any claim to objective knowledge dissolved. The intellectual tradition fractured into competing attempts to explain why human beings think and act as they do without reference to objective truth: Marx's material determinism, Freud's unconscious drives, Nietzsche's will to power. Each of these systems shared a common assumption — that what we call knowledge or morality or truth is not discovered but produced, not found but constructed through processes internal to human consciousness or certain cultural social structures.

But among these thinkers, Nietzsche deserves particular attention. He stands as the figure who most clearly diagnosed what had happened. He had the intellectual clarity to name the disease. He understood and revealed the spiritual emptiness and loss of meaning long before it became fully visible. Nietzsche diagnosed with brutal clarity that European culture had already abandoned God in practice while retaining His vocabulary. His famous "God is dead" was not a metaphysical claim but a cultural observation. As far as the actual functioning of knowledge, morality, and academic life were concerned, God's existence or non-existence made no difference. The institutions that claimed to operate under Christian truth were in fact operating under very different principles. They would focus more on human will, social power, and pragmatic utility. This is the intellectual heritage that shapes contemporary culture far more thoroughly than most recognize. The modern world has blindly accepted the core premises of constructivism: that reality as we know it is a construction of human minds, language, and social structures; that the identities and categories through which we organize experience reflect not objective distinctions but human purposes; that meaning is made rather than found. These assumptions pervade not only academic philosophy but popular consciousness. We inhabit a world where "your truth" and "my truth" are considered legitimate phrases, where moral claims are regularly dismissed as mere expressions of preference or power, where the question "who are you to judge?" functions as a conversation-ending phrase. The collapse of confidence in objective truth — the conviction that there exist facts about reality and morality that hold regardless of what anyone believes or desires — has not liberated us. It has trapped us in a closed circle of the self-referential will.

What happens when the will is trapped within this closed circle? Let's closely examine Nietzsche's answer. For him the will to power becomes the sole organizing principle of existence. Once you accept that the mind cannot grasp reality independently of itself, then the only remaining question is what internal principle determines how the mind organizes appearances. For Nietzsche, that principle is the instinctual drive to assert and expand power. Everything humans do — from science, art, religion, morality — expresses this fundamental will. What we call truth or holiness or goodness are merely expressions of the drive for power. Nietzsche applied this analysis ruthlessly, even to Christianity itself. The Judeo-Christian ethic, he argued, represents a sophisticated power play: weak people convinced the strong that strength itself was wicked, that the powerful should be ashamed of their power, that virtue resided in weakness. These are not transcendent truths but strategic inversions, slave morality deployed as a weapon against master morality. Only the perfection of individual will — life lived at maximum strength, intensity, and self-assertion — gives point to existence when truth has been exposed as construction. This is constructivism's logic taken to its fullest. If there is no objective reality to which the will can submit, then the will must turn back upon itself and find value in its own exercise. But here Nietzsche encountered a fatal problem that he could not solve.

The problem is that a will that feeds only on itself cannot grow. Nietzsche's solution of the perfection of individual will through self-assertion contains a hidden contradiction. All life, to develop and flourish, requires nourishment from beyond itself. Growth comes not from self-contemplation but from interaction with objective reality. The same principle holds for human will. The will develops through encounters with reality beyond itself, through discipline that bends desire toward objective standards, through the hard work of bringing one's inner life into alignment with external truth. But the constructivist framework makes this impossible. If reality is merely construction, there is nothing objective for the will to submit to, nothing beyond the self for the will to take in and be transformed by. The will has nowhere to go except back upon itself. And a will turned inward, feeding on its own assertion, does not grow stronger. This is not just theoretical speculation but empirical observation. Nietzsche himself, for all his brilliance in diagnosing the cultural disease, found no fulfillment in the cure he prescribed. No one has. The honest pursuit of will to power has been tested for over a century. It produces not freedom but a particular kind of imprisonment. The consciousness becomes trapped within its own constructions, unable to escape the closed loop of self-reference, increasingly desperate for stimulation because internal vitality has collapsed.

The basis of practical reason has shifted such that mere individual desire now serves as sufficient justification for action. What was once understood as a guide toward reality has collapsed into mere expression of preference. We employ, often unconsciously, a form of phenomenalism — the belief that all we can know are appearances, never reality itself. Since there is no objective truth behind what we perceive, all that remains is how we arrange and interpret those appearances according to our will. Reality becomes something we shape through perspective rather than something we discover through inquiry. This is presented as liberation, as the fullest expression of human freedom. The modern West celebrates freedom precisely in these terms. It celebrates freedom from external constraint, freedom from imposed truth, freedom to construct meaning according to individual will. But this conception of freedom contains a fatal inversion. Genuine freedom does not begin with assertion but with submission. It requires submission to reality, to truth, to what exists independently of our preferences. A will that cannot submit to anything beyond itself is not free. It is imprisoned within the circle of its own constructions, cut off from the very reality that could nourish its development.

The consequences of this framework extend beyond secular culture into the church itself. Christians living in contemporary society face an epistemological crisis that most have not consciously recognized. They inhabit the same constructivist assumptions as their secular neighbors while maintaining Christian vocabulary. The evidence of this collapse appears in a simple question. If someone asks how to become a good person, where would we direct them? Contemporary culture offers no accessible body of moral knowledge capable of answering this question with authority. The early Western universities that once existed to cultivate theological and moral understanding now treat ethics as preference, values as construction, virtue as subjective determination. The implicit answer our culture provides is Nietzschean in structure. Build your own moral framework. Determine your own values. The pursuit of individual fulfillment through self-determined meaning has become the only widely recognized conception of human flourishing.

This logic has infiltrated Christian thinking in ways that fundamentally compromise its coherence. Modern Christians approach the objective truths of scripture through the same constructivist methodology that defines secular culture. They look inward when attempting to discern moral teaching, treat subjective experience as authoritative in interpretation, confuse personal conviction with divine revelation. The framework of phenomenalism operates even in contexts that explicitly claim submission to objective truth. But here the system encounters the same problem it creates everywhere else. When Christians are told to look within themselves for moral guidance, to follow their heart, to trust their inner sense of what God is saying, they eventually confront an inescapable reality. They do not know what they want. The will, untrained by submission to objective truth and undeveloped through discipline, cannot generate the clarity it has been asked to provide.

Consider the contemporary debate over displaying the Ten Commandments in public spaces. The very terms of this controversy reveal how thoroughly constructivism has displaced any shared understanding of moral knowledge. Both advocates and opponents operate within a framework where the commandments function as symbols rather than truths. The argument centers on cultural identity and political signaling, not on whether the commandments describe moral reality. No one seriously proposes that students should be evaluated on whether they grasp the truth of these commandments in the way they are evaluated on their understanding of mathematics or social studies. The debate is purely symbolic because we have abandoned the category that would make it substantive. We no longer treat moral claims as knowledge claims. The question is not whether the commandments belong on courthouse walls but whether we can recover the conviction that moral truth exists independently of human construction, that there are ways of living that align with reality and ways that violate it, that such knowledge can be taught and learned and lived.

There exists an alternative tradition about the nature of mind and will, one that precedes the modern collapse and offers a way beyond it. This tradition runs from the Old and New Testaments through Augustine and Aquinas, finding its clearest articulation in the concept of intentionality. Intentionality refers to the fundamental openness of mind toward reality. The mind, in this tradition, does not construct the world it knows but reaches toward a world that exists independently. It inhabits reality through this directedness, this natural capacity to grasp what is genuinely other than itself. In the Christian development of this framework, the mind reaches not only toward created reality but toward God himself, who is always already present as the ground of both mind and world. Augustine described this as the "inner light" through which God enables the mind to know truth. The mind does not create reality through its own activity. It discovers reality through submission to what God has made and continues to sustain.

This understanding changes everything about how the will develops. If the mind can genuinely reach beyond itself toward objective reality, then the will is not condemned to feed on its own assertions. It can take in what is other, submit to what is real, grow through encounter with truth that exists independently of preference. The will develops not through self-assertion but through discipline oriented toward reality. Freedom comes not from constructing meaning but from aligning oneself with truth. As Dallas Willard observes, the pianist achieves freedom not by asserting autonomy over the keyboard but by submitting to its objective structure, learning its constraints, mastering its possibilities until what was obstacle becomes extension of the will itself. This is how human formation actually works and how it should be interpreted in society. The will grows through taking in reality beyond itself, through community with others who share commitment to objective truth, through practices that bend desire toward what is genuinely good rather than merely preferred.

For Christians, recovering from this crisis requires rejecting the premises that Nietzsche exposed. The task is not to argue more persuasively within the constructivist framework but to abandon that framework entirely. Christians must recover the conviction that mind reaches toward objective reality through intentionality, that moral and spiritual truth exists independently of human construction, that knowledge of God and His will is genuinely possible. This is not primarily about resolving denominational disputes over biblical interpretation. Such disagreements have always existed within Christianity and will continue to exist wherever finite minds attempt to grasp infinite truth. The crisis is more fundamental. We have reached a point where many Christians no longer believe that moral and spiritual truth can be known at all. Disagreement has collapsed into relativism. Different interpretations are treated not as competing claims about objective reality, each requiring evaluation against scripture and tradition and reason, but as equally valid personal truths. The language of "your truth" and "my truth" has infiltrated Christian discourse just as thoroughly as it dominates secular culture. Theological positions become matters of preference rather than knowledge, immune to critique because they are framed as personal construction rather than claims about reality.

Contemporary Christian discourse increasingly operates as if Nietzsche were correct. When theological disagreements are settled by appeals to personal experience rather than reasoned argument about what is actually true, when "this is what God is saying to me" functions as a conversation-ending move that cannot be questioned, when "your truth" and "my truth" replace "the truth," Christians have accepted the constructivist methodology they claim to reject. When diversity of interpretation is celebrated as inherently valuable rather than treated as a problem requiring resolution through careful inquiry, the framework of constructivism has achieved total victory. The solution is not to eliminate disagreement but to recover the categories that make substantive disagreement possible. Disagreement about truth presupposes that truth exists and can be known. When everything becomes "your truth" versus "my truth," there is nothing left to disagree about, only perspectives to acknowledge. The urgent task is recovering the framework of intentionality within which genuine theological inquiry can occur. This requires insisting that claims about God, scripture, and morality are knowledge claims subject to evaluation, not expressions of personal construction beyond critique.

What difference exists between taking our own thoughts as authoritative and replacing God as the practical authority in our lives? We claim to seek His will while treating our subjective sense as determinative. We speak of submission to God while making ourselves the judges of what counts as His word. This pattern is ancient. When Israel constructed the golden calf at Sinai, they did not abandon Yahweh explicitly. They declared a feast "to the Lord" while bowing before an idol of their own making. The calf represented not atheism but the reshaping of God according to human preference, the substitution of what they could construct and control for the God who existed independently and made demands they found inconvenient. Contemporary Christians may not fashion physical idols, but the pattern remains identical. We construct a God who affirms our interpretations, validates our experiences, confirms our preferences. When theological claims become immune to evaluation because they are framed as personal revelation, when "this is my truth" becomes impossible to critique, we have created a deity who cannot contradict us. We have made ourselves the final interpreters of divine will without appeal to anything beyond our own consciousness.

Scripture diagnoses this condition with further precision. Consider Judges 21:25 — "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes." This was not presented as liberation but as judgment, as the collapse of a society into fragmentation when shared standards disappeared. When there is no authoritative revelation, no body of knowledge that transcends individual preference, no truth accessible to all, society fragments into competing subjectivisms. Each person becomes their own measure of truth and goodness. This is precisely the condition constructivism produces. It is precisely the condition scripture presents as social and spiritual collapse. The path Nietzsche diagnosed as culturally triumphant, the framework contemporary Christians have unconsciously adopted, leads not to freedom but to the very fragmentation and meaninglessness that scripture warned against.

This epistemological collapse is the root cause of institutional dysfunction within Christian spaces. The crisis analyzed in the previous essay on campus ministry also stems directly from this problem. When constructivism becomes the unexamined framework, even explicitly Christian environments cannot form disciples capable of sustained faithfulness. One might be skeptical of aesthetic spiritual intensity precisely because, under constructivist assumptions, desire itself becomes sufficient reason for action. Emotional experience replaces rational conviction. The validation of subjective feeling substitutes for the formation of will through submission to objective truth. Campus ministry, rather than serving as a space where students engage in rigorous theological inquiry and formation in virtue, becomes another venue for the therapeutic affirmation of personal construction.

The solution is not to eliminate community or corporate worship but to recover their proper function. Christian gathering should exist for the collective pursuit of truth, for mutual accountability to scripture and tradition, for the formation of disciples through practices oriented toward objective reality. This requires intellectual rigor that contemporary Christian culture systematically avoids. It requires insisting that there are better and worse interpretations of scripture, that some theological positions are more adequate to reality than others, that formation in virtue follows discernible patterns rather than emerging spontaneously from authentic self-expression. This is difficult precisely because our culture is thoroughly Nietzschean. The claim that objective moral and spiritual truth exists and can be known provokes resistance even among professing Christians who have fully absorbed constructivist assumptions. But until Christians recognize that the crisis is not theological diversity but epistemological collapse, that the enemy is not other interpretations but the framework that makes all interpretation equally valid, no recovery is possible. The imprisoned will cannot free itself. It requires submission to a reality beyond its own constructions. That reality is either there to be known, or Nietzsche was right and we are trapped forever in perspectives we create but cannot escape.
